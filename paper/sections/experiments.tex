\subsection{How Strong Are Core Baselines and Calibrated ERA5?}
Three reference points frame the comparison: local temporal continuity (linear interpolation and LOCF), calibrated ERA5 direct substitution, and learned imputation without ERA5.

Fig.~\ref{fig:main-results} compares core baselines, ECBIT variants, and ERA5-augmented fair baselines. The core baselines establish two reference points. First, local continuity is useful for short gaps but degrades as blocks grow longer. Linear interpolation is therefore a strong sanity check for short outages, while last-observation-carried-forward is consistently weaker. Second, ERA5 direct substitution is competitive for long gaps, showing that the macro-scale reanalysis background already contains useful information even without neural conditioning.

The pattern-level behavior in Fig.~\ref{fig:round1-baselines} is more informative than the aggregate ranking alone. Linear interpolation has mean MAE 0.303 on short blocks but deteriorates to 0.483 on long blocks, because its only information source is the boundary behavior around the gap. ERA5 direct substitution is nearly flat across gap regimes, with MAE about 0.38 for short, medium, and long blocks. This is worse than interpolation on short gaps but better than interpolation on long gaps, which is exactly the regime where a physically coherent background field should help. SAITS is the strongest non-ERA5 baseline by MAE, with overall MAE 0.334, but it still rises from 0.252 on short blocks to 0.410 on long blocks. iTransformer achieves the lowest mean baseline RMSE, although the difference from SAITS is within one standard deviation. Both learned baselines indicate that temporal structure helps but does not close the gap to ERA5-conditioned block imputation.

Table~\ref{tab:era5-direct-calibration} audits the ERA5 direct baseline. The reported Round 1 setting uses train-only global mean-bias correction. Removing calibration degrades MAE from 0.381 to 0.387, while a train-only per-variable linear calibration improves it slightly to 0.378. A stronger station-month mean-bias calibration reduces ERA5-direct MAE to 0.270 by estimating station-, month-, and variable-specific AWS-minus-ERA5 offsets from training windows only. This calibrated ERA5 result is a strong non-neural macro-background adaptation baseline and narrows the neural-model margin substantially: the matched neural gains of 0.012--0.025 normalized MAE are statistically supported but operationally modest, so the practical value of neural conditioning is clearest when sparse AWS context and cross-variable consistency are worth the added deployment complexity.

Pressure and temperature have comparatively small boundary discrepancies, whereas wind speed and relative humidity show larger jumps, consistent with the failure-mode discussion that local wind and humidity are harder for point-sampled ERA5 to represent.

Table~\ref{tab:round1-baselines} confirms the no-ERA5 baseline ranking. SAITS has the lowest MAE (0.334), and iTransformer has the lowest mean RMSE (0.508), although the RMSE difference from SAITS (0.517) is within one standard deviation. Both remain worse than the ERA5-augmented neural variants reported below, indicating that the main gain is not simply a transformer-backbone effect.

Per-variable baseline errors also show why a single aggregate number is insufficient. In the completed Round 1 matrix, relative humidity and wind speed are consistently harder than pressure and temperature. This matches the observed missingness structure: RH and wind have higher missing burden and longer outage blocks in the selected AntAWS stations. The task is therefore not merely temporal smoothing; it requires recovery of variables whose station-side records are both noisier and more frequently absent.

\subsection{How Do ERA5-Augmented Architectures Compare?}
Table~\ref{tab:fair-era5-baselines} gives a matched comparison in which SAITS and iTransformer are evaluated both with and without ERA5 on the same 27 block-missing configurations. Adding ERA5 improves both baselines substantially. SAITS drops from 0.334 to 0.245 MAE, and iTransformer drops from 0.360 to 0.258 MAE. The paired tests in Table~\ref{tab:fair-era5-tests} show that both gains are large and significant after Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple}.

Table~\ref{tab:non-overlap-sensitivity} reports a strict test-window sensitivity check for the retained iTransformer with ERA5 checkpoints. This check is limited to that baseline because the Round 2 gated ECBIT checkpoint files were not retained in the result artifacts; it therefore audits the split/window-overlap concern for a strong ERA5-augmented neural baseline rather than introducing a new gated-ECBIT result. Restricting evaluation to the deterministic non-overlapping test subset changes the overall iTransformer with ERA5 MAE from 0.2581 to 0.2587, a paired shift of only +0.0006. This magnitude is negligible relative to the ERA5/no-ERA5 and block/MCAR curriculum effects, indicating that overlapping test windows are not driving the ERA5-augmented baseline conclusion.

These results suggest that the main difference is access to ERA5, not the choice of fusion architecture. SAITS+ERA5 achieves the lowest aggregate mean (0.245 MAE), but its advantage over gated ECBIT is not significant after Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:fair-era5-tests}). iTransformer+ERA5, concat ECBIT, and gated ECBIT are statistically tied at 0.258 MAE. SAITS+ERA5 also shows larger cross-configuration spread: MAE rises from 0.200 on short blocks to 0.299 on long blocks and from 0.192 at 20\% missingness to 0.289 at 60\%, driven by block regime and missing-rate difficulty rather than seed noise (within-cell seed SD = 0.018). Against station-month calibrated ERA5 direct substitution (Table~\ref{tab:station-month-neural-tests}), all neural variants are significantly better in matched tests, but the margins are small (0.012--0.025 MAE). Calibrated ERA5 is therefore the simplest reliable operational baseline; ERA5-augmented neural models add value by incorporating the remaining sparse AWS context.

\subsection{Does ERA5 Conditioning Improve Block Imputation?}
Table~\ref{tab:round2-ablations} and Fig.~\ref{fig:round2-ablations} show the main ablation result. Removing ERA5 increases block-missing test MAE from 0.258 to 0.346, a 34\% relative degradation against gated injection. This is the largest measured effect in the study and supports ERA5 conditioning as the main source of improvement.

The effect is stable across missing rates. For the gated model, mean MAE remains near 0.259, 0.257, and 0.257 at 20\%, 40\%, and 60\% artificial missingness, respectively. The no-ERA5 model degrades from 0.331 to 0.345 and 0.362 over the same rates. Thus, ERA5 conditioning is not only a constant offset in average performance; it also dampens the error growth that occurs when more of the local station context is removed.

Gated injection and concat/no-cross fusion are essentially tied, with mean MAE 0.258 in both cases. Gated and concat fusion do not differ significantly (Holm $p=0.553$; 95\% CI includes zero), whereas both ERA5-conditioned variants show much lower error than the no-ERA5 model with large paired effects. The main supported effect is ERA5-conditioned block training rather than a particular fusion form.

The per-variable ablation shows where ERA5 helps. With ERA5, temperature, wind speed, pressure, and specific humidity MAE are approximately 0.159, 0.424, 0.055, and 0.215. Removing ERA5 increases these values to 0.254, 0.576, 0.097, and 0.292. Relative humidity also improves, though less dramatically, from 0.510 without ERA5 to 0.436 with ERA5. \subsection{Does the ERA5 Benefit Increase for Longer Outages?}
The block-length sensitivity analysis directly tests whether ERA5 is most useful when local station context is weakest. Unlike the main curriculum, which samples block lengths uniformly within the short, medium, and long ranges, this analysis fixes the maximum outage horizon to 24, 72, and 216 h. These horizons correspond to representative short, medium, and long outage settings. At 40\% missingness, the ERA5/no-ERA5 MAE gap increases from 0.037 for 24 h blocks to 0.070 for 72 h blocks and 0.092 for 216 h blocks. The relative improvement grows from 13.8\% to 26.4\%. The 216 h horizon corresponds to 72 steps, the lower edge of the long block-length range defined in Section~2.6; the long-regime sampling upper bound is 240 steps, or 720 h.

(b) ERA5 value is concentrated in temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity. (c) Inference-time downsampling shows tolerance to 6-hour ERA5 inputs but not to daily inputs.}

The variable-level block-length results show that the long-gap degradation is not uniform. For 216-hour blocks, no-ERA5 wind-speed MAE reaches 0.586 and RH MAE reaches 0.519, while ERA5-conditioned wind-speed and RH MAE are 0.426 and 0.436. Temperature also shows a large long-gap benefit, dropping from 0.258 without ERA5 to 0.159 with ERA5. Pressure remains comparatively easy because both AWS and ERA5 pressure are smooth at synoptic scales. These results suggest that ERA5 is most valuable when the missing variable has strong synoptic evolution and when local interpolation cannot infer that evolution from endpoints alone.

\subsection{Which ERA5 Variables Matter?}
The variable-masking analysis in Fig.~\ref{fig:followup-analyses} shows that the ERA5 signal is concentrated in a small set of meteorological channels. Masking ERA5 temperature increases MAE by 0.0559, followed by wind speed at 0.0404 and specific humidity at 0.0374. Pressure and relative humidity produce smaller degradations. This supports a physically interpretable view of the conditioning signal: temperature, wind, and moisture background states carry most of the useful information for reconstructing missing AWS windows. Because AWS specific humidity is a derived benchmark channel, this result should be read as the value of an ERA5 moisture-state channel rather than as evidence for an independent in situ $q$ sensor.

This ordering is physically plausible. ERA5 temperature provides the large-scale thermal state and synoptic variability that local interpolation loses during multi-day outages. Wind speed is difficult to reconstruct from nearby AWS variables alone because it is strongly affected by pressure gradients, katabatic forcing, local topography, and coastal flow regimes. Specific humidity contributes moisture-state information that is more stable than relative humidity under temperature-dependent saturation effects. By contrast, ERA5 pressure is already highly correlated with AWS pressure and is often smooth enough for station-side temporal context to recover. The smaller relative-humidity contribution is treated as a benchmark-specific result rather than as a general ERA5 humidity-validation claim.

The masking experiment is an inference-time sensitivity test, not a retraining ablation: each perturbation replaces one ERA5 channel with its training-set mean while leaving the trained model unchanged. It therefore measures the model's learned reliance on each channel rather than the effect of excluding that channel during training. The top three channels account for most of the total masking degradation, and the computational cost is negligible compared with retraining, making the analysis a practical diagnostic when full ERA5 processing is unavailable.

\subsection{Is High-Frequency ERA5 Required?}
The temporal downsampling analysis shows how checkpoints trained with 3-hourly ERA5 respond to coarser ERA5 inputs at inference time. Downsampling ERA5 to 6-hour resolution increases MAE by only 0.0008, and 12-hour resolution increases MAE by 0.0073. Daily downsampling is more damaging, increasing MAE by 0.0302. This suggests that the trained model mainly exploits mesoscale background evolution rather than hour-level fluctuations, but daily inputs are too coarse for the current setup.

ERA5 is available hourly, while the AntAWS product is 3-hourly. The near-zero penalty at 6 h resolution indicates that ECBIT can be deployed with sub-daily ERA5 inputs without retraining. Daily averages, however, remove synoptic timing, diurnal structure, and rapid frontal transitions that are informative during long outages.

\subsection{Cross-Paradigm Comparison of Curriculum and ERA5 Conditioning}
Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-era5-factorial} compares training-mask distribution and ERA5 conditioning on a common block-missing test distribution. The MCAR-trained rows are deliberately out-of-distribution evaluations on block-missing tests, so they measure block-test transfer failure rather than ordinary in-distribution model performance. The best cell is block training with ERA5 conditioning, with overall MAE 0.258. Removing ERA5 under the same block curriculum increases MAE to 0.346, giving the cleanest estimate of ERA5 value under the target failure mode: 0.088 MAE. Conversely, keeping ERA5 but training with MCAR masks gives MAE 0.407 on block tests. The worst cell is MCAR training without ERA5, with MAE 0.529.

\input{tables/tab_curriculum_era5_factorial}

The table is best read as a block-test transfer analysis rather than an averaged main-effects decomposition. Under the target block curriculum, ERA5 reduces overall MAE by 0.088. Under ERA5 conditioning, changing the training mask from MCAR to block reduces block-test MAE from 0.407 to 0.258. This row difference reflects failure-mode matching: the model must learn how to use ERA5 when the AWS variable is absent for a contiguous interval, not only when isolated points are missing.

The matrix shows that neither ERA5 access nor block training is sufficient on its own. MCAR+ERA5 still performs poorly on block tests, while block training without ERA5 remains worse than block training with ERA5. For this controlled ECBIT transfer matrix, the lowest block-test error requires both the realistic failure-mode curriculum and the external physical prior.

This result also explains why MCAR+ERA5 is worse than block training without ERA5. Under MCAR masks, the model usually sees scattered local AWS context at the same or adjacent time steps and can use ERA5 as an instantaneous auxiliary value. Under block failures, the required behavior is different: the model must follow the temporal evolution of ERA5 across a multi-day interval where the target AWS variable is absent. MCAR training does not teach this mode of use; it rewards local patching around isolated missing points rather than sustained reliance on the reanalysis trajectory. Consequently, even an ERA5-equipped MCAR model is less effective on block tests than a no-ERA5 model trained to reconstruct contiguous outages from station-side temporal and cross-variable context.

\subsection{How Well Does the Model Generalize Across Stations?}
Held-out evaluation reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity. Mean MAE ranges from 0.173 on Nico to 0.350 on Mount Sidley. This variation is too large to hide behind a single aggregate score and likely reflects differences in station environment, elevation, coastal versus interior regimes, and station-specific ERA5 bias.

Table~\ref{tab:heldout-station-detail} expands this result by station and variable. The largest held-out errors are concentrated in relative humidity and wind speed, whereas pressure remains comparatively easy. This mirrors the main-test variable pattern and indicates that transfer difficulty is not uniform across meteorological channels.

Mount Sidley and Zhongshan illustrate two different hard cases. In the AntAWS metadata used here, the Mount Sidley AWS is installed at 2123 m on a high-elevation West Antarctic volcanic massif. Point-sampled ERA5 may therefore misrepresent near-surface temperature and wind conditions even when the synoptic evolution is correct, because local orography and boundary-layer structure can differ from the reanalysis grid representation. Zhongshan is a low-elevation coastal station, where sea-ice, ocean, and katabatic-flow interactions can introduce mesoscale variability that is difficult to capture at ERA5 grid scale \citep{tetzner2019era5antarcticpeninsula,catonharrison2022antarcticwinds}. Fig.~\ref{fig:heldout-bias} provides a diagnostic view: the two highest-error held-out stations also have relatively large ERA5-AWS mismatch. With five held-out stations, this pattern is useful for identifying difficult cases rather than for estimating a population-level correlation.

The held-out pattern also clarifies what should be expected from operational deployment. Low MAE at Nico indicates that some unseen stations are well served by the learned main-station representation. Higher MAE at Mount Sidley and Zhongshan indicates that metadata and local regime information may be needed at difficult sites. Future benchmark reports should therefore include station-level tables, not only pooled test scores, because the same aggregate performance can hide very different station-level reliability profiles.
