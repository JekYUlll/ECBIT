\subsection{How Strong Are Non-ERA5 Baselines?}
The evidence supports ERA5-conditioned block imputation, but it does not support the stronger claim that a particular fusion layer is necessary. This distinction is important for interpreting all result tables below.

Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} summarizes the main comparison across completed baselines, ECBIT variants, and ERA5-augmented fair baselines. The core baselines establish two reference points. First, local continuity is useful for short gaps but degrades as blocks grow longer. Linear interpolation is therefore a strong sanity check for short outages, while last-observation-carried-forward is consistently weaker. Second, ERA5 direct substitution is competitive for long gaps, showing that reanalysis already contains useful background information even without neural conditioning.

The pattern-level behavior in Fig.~\ref{fig:round1-baselines} is more informative than the aggregate ranking alone. Linear interpolation has mean MAE 0.303 on short blocks but deteriorates to 0.483 on long blocks, because its only information source is the boundary behavior around the gap. ERA5 direct substitution is nearly flat across gap regimes, with MAE about 0.38 for short, medium, and long blocks. This is worse than interpolation on short gaps but better than interpolation on long gaps, which is exactly the regime where a physically coherent background field should help. SAITS is the strongest non-ERA5 baseline by MAE, with overall MAE 0.334, but it still rises from 0.252 on short blocks to 0.410 on long blocks. iTransformer achieves the lowest mean baseline RMSE, although the difference from SAITS is within one standard deviation. Both learned baselines indicate that temporal structure helps but does not close the gap to ERA5-conditioned block imputation.

Because ERA5 direct substitution is an important physical-reference baseline, Table~\ref{tab:era5-direct-calibration} audits its calibration. The reported Round 1 baseline uses train-only global mean-bias correction. Removing calibration degrades MAE from 0.381 to 0.387, while a train-only per-variable linear calibration improves it slightly to 0.378. A stronger station-month mean-bias calibration reduces ERA5-direct MAE to 0.270 by estimating station-, month-, and variable-specific AWS-minus-ERA5 offsets from training windows only. This is a strong and operationally simple reference: station-season bias correction explains a large fraction of the recoverable ERA5 signal and narrows the gap to ERA5-augmented neural models. The remaining value of neural conditioning should therefore be interpreted as a modest gain from combining ERA5 trajectories with sparse station-side temporal and cross-variable context under variable-wise block outages, not as a broad replacement of calibrated ERA5.

This audit is not a supervised accuracy evaluation because AWS ground truth is unavailable inside naturally missing gaps. It only checks whether ERA5-based filling connects to the available AWS boundaries at a plausible scale. Pressure and temperature have comparatively small boundary discrepancies, whereas wind speed and relative humidity show larger jumps, consistent with the failure-mode discussion that local wind and humidity are harder for point-sampled ERA5 to represent.

SAITS has the lowest no-ERA5 baseline MAE (0.334). iTransformer achieves the lowest mean no-ERA5 baseline RMSE (0.508), though the difference from SAITS (0.517) is within one standard deviation. Both remain substantially worse than all ERA5-augmented neural variants in the matched block-missing setting reported below. This gap indicates that the main improvement is not simply from using a transformer-style imputation backbone; it comes from conditioning on external meteorological background information.

Per-variable baseline errors also show why a single aggregate number is insufficient. In the completed Round 1 matrix, relative humidity and wind speed are consistently harder than pressure and temperature. This matches the observed missingness structure: RH and wind have higher missing burden and longer outage blocks in the selected AntAWS stations. The task is therefore not merely temporal smoothing; it requires recovery of variables whose station-side records are both noisier and more frequently absent.

\subsection{Do ERA5-Augmented Baselines Match ECBIT?}
Table~\ref{tab:fair-era5-baselines} gives the fair comparison requested by the baseline analysis: SAITS and iTransformer are evaluated both with and without ERA5 on the same 27 block-missing configurations. Adding ERA5 improves both baselines substantially. SAITS drops from 0.334 to 0.245 MAE, and iTransformer drops from 0.360 to 0.258 MAE. The paired tests in Table~\ref{tab:fair-era5-tests} confirm that both gains are large and significant after Holm correction.

\input{tables/tab_fair_era5_baselines}

\input{tables/tab_fair_era5_tests}

These results change the interpretation of the architecture claim. SAITS+ERA5 has the lowest aggregate mean MAE (0.245), but its configuration-to-configuration standard deviation is much larger than the variate-token models, and its paired difference from gated ECBIT is not significant after Holm correction. iTransformer+ERA5, concat ECBIT, and gated ECBIT are effectively tied near 0.258 MAE. Table~\ref{tab:station-month-neural-tests} adds the complementary comparison against station-month calibrated ERA5 direct substitution. The neural variants are significantly better than the calibrated direct baseline in matched tests, but the mean differences are small for the variate-token models. Thus, the completed fair baselines do not support a claim that ECBIT's gated injection is uniquely superior to all ERA5-augmented imputation architectures. They instead show that calibrated ERA5 is already a strong operational baseline, while ERA5-augmented neural models provide a smaller additional gain when they can use the remaining AWS context.

\subsection{Does ERA5 Conditioning Improve Block Imputation?}
Table~\ref{tab:round2-ablations} and Fig.~\ref{fig:round2-ablations} show the main ablation result. Removing ERA5 increases block-missing test MAE from 0.258 to 0.346, a 34\% relative degradation against gated injection. This is the largest effect in the study and supports ERA5 as the dominant source of improvement.

The effect is stable across missing rates. For the gated model, mean MAE remains near 0.259, 0.257, and 0.257 at 20\%, 40\%, and 60\% artificial missingness, respectively. The no-ERA5 model degrades from 0.331 to 0.345 and 0.362 over the same rates. Thus, ERA5 conditioning is not only a constant offset in average performance; it also dampens the error growth that occurs when more of the local station context is removed.

The same ablation also prevents an over-interpretation of the architecture. Gated injection and concat/no-cross fusion are essentially tied, with mean MAE 0.258 in both cases. Table~\ref{tab:stat-tests} reports paired tests over the matched 27 configurations, including 95\% confidence intervals, paired standardized effects, and Holm correction over the three comparisons. Gated and concat fusion do not differ significantly (Holm $p=0.553$; 95\% CI includes zero), whereas both ERA5-conditioned variants strongly outperform the no-ERA5 model with large paired effects. Therefore, the evidence supports ERA5-conditioned block imputation, but it does not support the stronger claim that a particular fusion layer is necessary.

Because the decoder emits a full reconstructed trajectory, we separately audit whether visible input values are modified before final post-processing. Table~\ref{tab:observed-consistency} uses the retained MCAR no-ERA5 ECBIT checkpoints as a direct check. The decoded trajectory can deviate from visible inputs before post-processing, but the final copy-back rule sets observed-position error to exactly zero. The per-variable ablation reinforces this conclusion. With ERA5, temperature, wind speed, pressure, and specific humidity MAE are approximately 0.159, 0.424, 0.055, and 0.215. Removing ERA5 increases these values to 0.254, 0.576, 0.097, and 0.292. Relative humidity also improves, though less dramatically, from 0.510 without ERA5 to 0.436 with ERA5. The largest absolute improvements occur in physically coherent background variables: temperature, wind, and moisture. This pattern is more important than the small numerical difference between gated and concat fusion.

Table~\ref{tab:raw-unit-errors} reports the same gated ERA5 result in physical units. The normalized aggregate corresponds to mean absolute errors of 2.14 deg C for temperature, 2.25 m s$^{-1}$ for wind speed, 4.91 hPa for pressure, 8.85 percentage points for relative humidity, and 0.15 g kg$^{-1}$ for specific humidity. These values are intended as scale interpretation rather than a replacement for normalized comparisons, because the benchmark optimizes a multi-variable objective.

\subsection{Does the ERA5 Benefit Increase for Longer Outages?}
The block-length sensitivity analysis directly tests whether ERA5 is most useful when local station context is weakest. Unlike the main curriculum, which samples random lengths within the short, medium, and long ranges, this analysis fixes the maximum outage horizon to 24, 72, and 216 hours. These horizons correspond to representative short, medium, and long outage settings; the 216-hour setting is intentionally inside the long range of 72--240 hours rather than at its upper bound. At 40\% missingness, the ERA5/no-ERA5 MAE gap increases from 0.037 for 24-hour blocks to 0.070 for 72-hour blocks and 0.092 for 216-hour blocks. The relative improvement grows from 13.8\% to 26.4\%.

(b) ERA5 value is concentrated in temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity. (c) The model is robust to 6-hour ERA5 inputs but not to daily downsampling.}
  \label{fig:followup-analyses}

This monotonic pattern is important for the Antarctic AWS setting. It shows that ERA5 is not only improving average error; it is most helpful in the long contiguous outages that motivate the benchmark.

The variable-level block-length results show that the long-gap degradation is not uniform. For 216-hour blocks, no-ERA5 wind-speed MAE reaches 0.586 and RH MAE reaches 0.519, while ERA5-conditioned wind-speed and RH MAE are 0.426 and 0.436. Temperature also shows a large long-gap benefit, dropping from 0.258 without ERA5 to 0.159 with ERA5. Pressure remains comparatively easy because both AWS and ERA5 pressure are smooth at synoptic scales. These results suggest that ERA5 is most valuable when the missing variable has strong synoptic evolution and when local interpolation cannot infer that evolution from endpoints alone.

\subsection{Which ERA5 Variables Matter?}
The variable-masking analysis in Fig.~\ref{fig:followup-analyses} shows that the ERA5 signal is concentrated in a small set of meteorological channels. Masking ERA5 temperature increases MAE by 0.0559, followed by wind speed at 0.0404 and specific humidity at 0.0374. Pressure and relative humidity produce smaller degradations. This supports a physically interpretable view of the conditioning signal: temperature, wind, and moisture background states carry most of the useful information for reconstructing missing AWS windows.

This ordering is physically plausible. ERA5 temperature provides the large-scale thermal state and synoptic variability that local interpolation loses during multi-day outages. Wind speed is difficult to reconstruct from nearby AWS variables alone because it is strongly affected by pressure gradients, katabatic forcing, local topography, and coastal flow regimes. Specific humidity contributes moisture-state information that is more stable than relative humidity under temperature-dependent saturation effects. By contrast, ERA5 pressure is already highly correlated with AWS pressure and is often smooth enough for station-side temporal context to recover; ERA5 relative humidity is useful but less reliable because polar humidity biases and near-saturation behavior can be difficult for reanalysis to represent.

The masking experiment is an inference-time sensitivity test, not a retraining ablation. Each perturbation replaces one ERA5 channel with its training-set mean while leaving the trained model unchanged. This design is computationally efficient because it reuses the 27 trained checkpoints and avoids retraining five channel-ablated models. It therefore measures how much the trained ECBIT relies on each ERA5 channel under the learned conditioning policy. The fact that the top three channels account for most of the total masking degradation gives a practical variable-prioritization rule for deployments where full ERA5 processing is not always available.

\subsection{Is High-Frequency ERA5 Required?}
The temporal downsampling analysis shows that the model is robust to moderate ERA5 temporal coarsening. Downsampling ERA5 to 6-hour resolution increases MAE by only 0.0008, and 12-hour resolution increases MAE by 0.0073. Daily downsampling is more damaging, increasing MAE by 0.0302. This suggests that the model mainly exploits mesoscale background evolution rather than fragile hour-level variation, but daily inputs are too coarse for the current training setup.

This result matters operationally. ERA5 is available hourly, while the AntAWS product used here is 3-hourly. The near-zero penalty at 6-hour resolution indicates that ECBIT does not depend on exact hour-by-hour ERA5 fluctuations; it can use a coarser but still sub-daily background trajectory. The larger 24-hour penalty indicates the lower limit of that robustness. Daily averages remove synoptic timing, diurnal structure, and rapid frontal transitions that are useful during long AWS outages. For practical gap filling, the result supports delayed or lower-frequency ERA5 pipelines as long as they preserve sub-daily evolution.

\subsection{Cross-Paradigm Comparison of Curriculum and ERA5 Conditioning}
Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-era5-factorial} compares training-mask distribution and ERA5 conditioning on a common block-missing test distribution. This table should not be interpreted as a classical balanced factorial design with all cells evaluated in their native training distribution. The MCAR-trained rows are deliberately out-of-distribution evaluations on block-missing tests, and therefore measure block-test transfer failure rather than ordinary in-distribution model performance. The best cell is block training with ERA5 conditioning, with overall MAE 0.258. Removing ERA5 under the same block curriculum increases MAE to 0.346, giving the cleanest estimate of ERA5 value under the target failure mode: 0.088 MAE. Conversely, keeping ERA5 but training with MCAR masks gives MAE 0.407 on block tests. The worst cell is MCAR training without ERA5, with MAE 0.529.

\input{tables/tab_curriculum_era5_factorial}

The key comparison is therefore not a formal decomposition into averaged main effects, but a block-test transfer analysis. Under the target block curriculum, ERA5 reduces overall MAE by 0.088. Under ERA5 conditioning, changing the training mask from MCAR to block reduces block-test MAE from 0.407 to 0.258. This row difference reflects failure-mode matching: the model must learn how to use ERA5 when the AWS variable is absent for a contiguous interval, not only when isolated points are missing.

The table is the most important safeguard against a misleading interpretation of the paper. A simple comparison between no-ERA5 and ERA5-conditioned variants could suggest that the result is just an auxiliary-data effect. A simple in-distribution MCAR score could suggest that random masks are sufficient. The cross-paradigm design shows that neither explanation is complete: for this controlled ECBIT transfer matrix, the lowest block-test error requires both the realistic failure-mode curriculum and the external physical prior.

This result also explains why MCAR+ERA5 is worse than block training without ERA5. Under MCAR masks, the model usually sees scattered local AWS context at the same or adjacent time steps and can use ERA5 as an instantaneous auxiliary value. Under block failures, the required behavior is different: the model must follow the temporal evolution of ERA5 across a multi-day interval where the target AWS variable is absent. MCAR training does not teach this mode of use; it rewards local patching around isolated missing points rather than sustained reliance on the reanalysis trajectory. Consequently, even an ERA5-equipped MCAR model is less effective on block tests than a no-ERA5 model trained to reconstruct contiguous outages from station-side temporal and cross-variable context.

\subsection{How Well Does the Model Generalize Across Stations?}
Held-out station evaluation reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity. Mean MAE ranges from 0.173 on Nico to 0.350 on Mount Sidley. This variation is too large to hide behind a single aggregate score and likely reflects differences in station environment, elevation, coastal versus interior regimes, and station-specific ERA5 bias.

Table~\ref{tab:heldout-station-detail} expands this result by station and variable. The largest held-out errors are concentrated in relative humidity and wind speed, whereas pressure remains comparatively easy. This mirrors the main-test variable pattern and indicates that transfer difficulty is not uniform across meteorological channels.

  \caption{Held-out station imputation MAE versus ERA5-AWS mismatch. ERA5-AWS mismatch is computed on observed positions in each held-out station's chronological training segment and is used only as a diagnostic. With only five held-out stations, the trend should be interpreted cautiously.}
  \label{fig:heldout-bias}

Mount Sidley and Zhongshan illustrate two different hard cases. In the AntAWS metadata used here, the Mount Sidley AWS is installed at 2123 m on a high-elevation West Antarctic volcanic massif. Point-sampled ERA5 may therefore misrepresent near-surface temperature and wind conditions even when the synoptic evolution is correct, because local orography and boundary-layer structure can differ from the reanalysis grid representation. Zhongshan is a low-elevation coastal station, where sea-ice, ocean, and katabatic-flow interactions can introduce mesoscale variability that is difficult to capture at ERA5 grid scale. Figure~\ref{fig:heldout-bias} provides a diagnostic view: although the held-out sample is small, stations with larger ERA5-AWS mismatch tend to have higher imputation error (Pearson $r=0.66$ over five stations). This supports the interpretation that station-specific ERA5 bias is one factor in transfer difficulty, but it should not be read as a formal statistical claim.

The held-out pattern also clarifies what should be expected from operational deployment. Low MAE at Nico indicates that some unseen stations are well served by the learned main-station representation. Higher MAE at Mount Sidley and Zhongshan indicates that metadata and local regime information may be needed for robust deployment at difficult sites. Future benchmark reports should therefore include station-level tables, not only pooled test scores, because the same aggregate performance can hide very different station-level reliability profiles.
