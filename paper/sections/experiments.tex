\subsection{How Strong Are Non-ERA5 Baselines?}
Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} summarizes the main comparison across completed baselines and ECBIT variants. The core baselines establish two reference points. First, local continuity is useful for short gaps but degrades as blocks grow longer. Linear interpolation is therefore a strong sanity check for short outages, while last-observation-carried-forward is consistently weaker. Second, ERA5 direct substitution is competitive for long gaps, showing that reanalysis already contains useful background information even without neural conditioning.

  \caption{Qualitative long-block imputation case for AWS temperature. The shaded region marks artificially hidden observations used for evaluation. Linear interpolation is constrained by endpoint continuity, whereas ERA5 provides a physically plausible background trajectory through the outage.}
  \label{fig:imputation-case}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:imputation-case} illustrates why long contiguous outages require external context. When observations are absent for multiple days, local interpolation becomes a low-frequency bridge between endpoints, while ERA5 retains synoptic-scale temperature evolution within the missing interval. The figure is a qualitative diagnostic; the model comparisons below use aggregate test metrics over all stations, missing rates, seeds, and block regimes.

The iTransformer imputation baseline is the strongest completed baseline on average, with mean MAE 0.360. However, it remains substantially worse than ERA5-conditioned ECBIT in the matched block-missing setting reported below. This gap indicates that the main improvement is not simply from using a variate-token transformer backbone; it comes from conditioning on external meteorological background information.

\subsection{Does ERA5 Conditioning Improve Block Imputation?}
Table~\ref{tab:round2-ablations} and Fig.~\ref{fig:round2-ablations} show the main ablation result. Removing ERA5 increases block-missing test MAE from 0.258 to 0.346, a 34\% relative degradation against gated injection. This is the largest effect in the study and supports ERA5 as the dominant source of improvement.

The same ablation also prevents an over-interpretation of the architecture. Gated injection and concat/no-cross fusion are essentially tied, with mean MAE 0.258 in both cases. Therefore, the evidence supports ERA5-conditioned block imputation, but it does not support the stronger claim that a particular fusion layer is necessary.

\subsection{Does the ERA5 Benefit Increase for Longer Outages?}
The block-length sensitivity analysis directly tests whether ERA5 is most useful when local station context is weakest. At 40\% missingness, the ERA5/no-ERA5 MAE gap increases from 0.037 for 24-hour blocks to 0.070 for 72-hour blocks and 0.092 for 216-hour blocks. The relative improvement grows from 13.8\% to 26.4\%.

This monotonic pattern is important for the Antarctic AWS setting. It shows that ERA5 is not only improving average error; it is most helpful in the long contiguous outages that motivate the benchmark.

\subsection{Which ERA5 Variables Matter?}
The variable-masking analysis in Fig.~\ref{fig:followup-analyses} shows that the ERA5 signal is concentrated in a small set of meteorological channels. Masking ERA5 temperature increases MAE by 0.0559, followed by wind speed at 0.0404 and specific humidity at 0.0374. Pressure and relative humidity produce smaller degradations. This supports a physically interpretable view of the conditioning signal: temperature, wind, and moisture background states carry most of the useful information for reconstructing missing AWS windows.

\subsection{Is High-Frequency ERA5 Required?}
The temporal downsampling analysis shows that the model is robust to moderate ERA5 temporal coarsening. Downsampling ERA5 to 6-hour resolution increases MAE by only 0.0008, and 12-hour resolution increases MAE by 0.0073. Daily downsampling is more damaging, increasing MAE by 0.0302. This suggests that the model mainly exploits mesoscale background evolution rather than fragile hour-level variation, but daily inputs are too coarse for the current training setup.

\subsection{Factorial Ablation of Curriculum and ERA5 Conditioning}
Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-era5-factorial} completes the factorial check between training-mask distribution and ERA5 conditioning. The best cell is block training with ERA5 conditioning, with overall MAE 0.258. Removing ERA5 under the same block curriculum increases MAE to 0.346. Conversely, keeping ERA5 but training with MCAR masks gives MAE 0.407 on block tests. The worst cell is MCAR training without ERA5, with MAE 0.529.

\input{tables/tab_curriculum_era5_factorial}

The factorized effect sizes show that block curriculum is the larger main effect. Averaged over ERA5 settings, changing MCAR training to block training reduces MAE by 0.166. Averaged over training curricula, adding ERA5 reduces MAE by 0.105. The difference-in-differences is positive but modest: ERA5 improves MCAR training by 0.122 MAE and block training by 0.088 MAE, corresponding to an effect-coded interaction of 0.017 MAE. Thus, the result is best described as two strong independent main effects with a small positive synergy, not as a purely architectural interaction.

This result also explains why MCAR+ERA5 is worse than block training without ERA5. Under MCAR masks, the model sees scattered local context and can use ERA5 as an instantaneous auxiliary value. Under block failures, the required behavior is different: the model must use the temporal evolution of ERA5 across a long interval where AWS evidence is absent. ERA5 is therefore useful only when the training objective teaches the model to rely on it under contiguous outages.

\subsection{How Well Does the Model Generalize Across Stations?}
Held-out station evaluation reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity. Mean MAE ranges from 0.173 on Nico to 0.350 on Mount Sidley. This variation is too large to hide behind a single aggregate score and likely reflects differences in station environment, elevation, coastal versus interior regimes, and station-specific ERA5 bias.

The held-out results motivate future station-adaptive conditioning, but they do not invalidate the main result. They show that ERA5-conditioned block imputation transfers to unseen stations with variable difficulty, and that station-level reporting should be part of Antarctic AWS imputation benchmarks.
