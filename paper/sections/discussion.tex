\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the ERA5 Signal Represents}
The results support a specific interpretation of ERA5 conditioning. ERA5 is not merely another correlated input channel that improves every setting equally. Its value grows as station outages become longer, precisely when local interpolation and station-history cues weaken. This is consistent with ERA5 acting as a physically coherent background state during multi-day sensor failures.

The variable-masking analysis further shows that the ERA5 contribution is not uniform across variables. Temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity produce the largest MAE increases when removed. Relative humidity is least important among the tested channels in this benchmark, consistent with the broader result that ERA5 value depends strongly on variable and station context. This supports the decision to condition on ERA5 through missing-variable gates rather than replacing station observations wholesale.

This interpretation is consistent with the broader reanalysis-evaluation literature. ERA5 captures large-scale atmospheric evolution, but Antarctic station-scale fidelity depends on surface type, coastal geometry, elevation, and boundary-layer processes \cite{zhu2021era5antarctica,tetzner2019era5antarcticpeninsula,catonharrison2022antarcticwinds}. ECBIT therefore treats ERA5 as a structured prior with conditional value. The model is not asked to decide whether ERA5 is universally better than AWS; AWS remains the label source whenever observed. ERA5 only becomes useful when AWS evidence is absent, and even then its usefulness differs by variable.

\subsection{Why Complex Fusion Did Not Dominate}
The gated-injection, concat/no-cross, and ERA5-augmented baseline experiments show that the specific fusion mechanism is not the dominant source of improvement. Gated injection and concat/no-cross fusion are not significantly different in paired tests, and iTransformer+ERA5 is also tied with gated ECBIT. SAITS+ERA5 has a lower aggregate mean, but the paired confidence interval against gated ECBIT crosses zero after Holm correction. Once aligned ERA5 information and block-missing training are available, several lightweight conditioning mechanisms are competitive on the present benchmark.

The method should therefore be read as a controlled test of when external reanalysis becomes useful for Antarctic AWS imputation, rather than as evidence that attention-style fusion is intrinsically superior. This narrower interpretation makes the empirical result stronger: the benchmark identifies the value of aligned ERA5 access and failure-mode-matched training without relying on a fragile architecture ranking.

The result also provides a practical lesson for exogenous time-series modeling. Emerging fusion studies suggest that auxiliary information needs constraints, selection, or balancing rather than direct unconstrained mixing \cite{lee2026constrainedfusion,cao2026ecto,chen2025exost}. ECBIT reaches a compatible conclusion in a polar meteorological setting: once the external source is temporally aligned and physically relevant, the exact fusion layer contributes little relative to the data and curriculum design. Better metadata-aware conditioning, station-specific calibration, and uncertainty-aware ERA5 use are likely to matter more than more elaborate attention modules.

\subsection{Curriculum and Failure-Mode Matching}
The MCAR-on-block evaluation shows that ERA5 conditioning alone is insufficient. A model trained with MCAR masks performs poorly when evaluated on block-missing tests, even though it has access to ERA5. This indicates an interaction between the external physical prior and the missingness curriculum: the model must learn to use ERA5 under contiguous outage conditions, not only under randomly scattered missing points.

For practical Antarctic AWS recovery, this matters more than performance on generic random-missing benchmarks. Station failures often remove contiguous spans of one or more variables, so evaluation and training should match that failure mode.

The completed block-test transfer matrix makes this conclusion quantitative without treating the MCAR rows as in-distribution factorial cells. Under block training, ERA5 reduces MAE by 0.088. With ERA5 present, changing from MCAR masks to block masks reduces block-test MAE from 0.407 to 0.258. This is methodologically useful beyond the present dataset: exogenous variables can be valuable, but their value depends on whether the training mask distribution teaches the model how to use them under the target failure mode.

This principle should generalize to other station networks. Polar AWS records, mountain weather stations, marine buoys, and remote ecological sensor networks often fail in contiguous blocks rather than independent points. In all such settings, a model trained on MCAR masks may learn a local interpolation problem that is easier than the deployment problem. A block curriculum changes the task from local patching to reconstruction under sustained evidence loss. ECBIT demonstrates this gap quantitatively for Antarctic AWS records, but the evaluation principle is broader: imputation benchmarks should simulate the dominant operational failure mode before ranking architectures.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Considerations}
The temporal-resolution analysis suggests that ECBIT does not require fragile hour-level ERA5 structure. Downsampling ERA5 to 6-hour resolution changes MAE by less than 0.001, while daily inputs are clearly too coarse. This supports practical deployments where only lower-frequency reanalysis products or delayed operational reanalysis streams are available, provided that sub-daily temporal structure is preserved.

The variable-importance results also suggest a prioritization rule for partial ERA5 availability. Temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity are the most valuable channels and account for about 84\% of the total single-channel masking degradation; pressure and relative humidity contribute less in the current benchmark. If a deployment pipeline must reduce ERA5 variables, the top three channels should be preserved first.

The held-out station results further suggest that deployment should include station-level validation. Mean MAE ranges from 0.173 to 0.350 across the five held-out stations, so high-elevation, coastal, or locally complex stations should be checked before operational use rather than assumed to follow the aggregate benchmark average.

A deployment pipeline should expose uncertainty or quality flags alongside filled values. The present work reports deterministic MAE/RMSE, but operational users may need to know when an imputed value is supported by strong ERA5 context and when it comes from a station whose local setting is poorly represented by point-sampled reanalysis. Real-time use also depends on ERA5 product availability, latency, station metadata quality, and preprocessing robustness.

The station-month calibrated ERA5 baseline is also practically important. Its 0.270 MAE shows that a simple train-only station-season bias correction is a strong operational baseline and should be reported in future AWS imputation studies. Neural ERA5 conditioning is still useful in the matched tests, but its role is more specific: it adds sparse AWS temporal and cross-variable context on top of the calibrated reanalysis trajectory.

\subsection{Station Heterogeneity}
Held-out station results vary by roughly a factor of two, from Nico to Mount Sidley. This heterogeneity likely reflects a combination of local geography, elevation, coastal versus interior climate regimes, and station-specific ERA5 representativeness. Mount Sidley has the largest held-out MAE. In the selected AntAWS metadata it is also the highest held-out AWS installation, at 2123 m, on the Mount Sidley volcanic massif. This number refers to the station metadata elevation, not the summit elevation of Mount Sidley itself. The station lies in West Antarctic complex terrain where coarse reanalysis orography and boundary-layer structure can be mismatched to the local AWS footprint. Zhongshan has the second-largest MAE despite its low elevation, plausibly because its coastal setting is affected by sea-ice, ocean, and local wind interactions that are not fully resolved by point-sampled ERA5. Future work should therefore consider station-adaptive conditioning, metadata-aware calibration, or few-shot adaptation for stations with large ERA5 bias.

\subsection{Failure Modes and Error Analysis}
The dominant error modes follow from the same evidence. First, very long gaps remain difficult when ERA5 itself is locally biased. ECBIT can follow the ERA5 temporal trajectory, but it cannot fully correct an elevation, coastal, or boundary-layer mismatch without additional station-specific information. Second, wind speed and relative humidity remain difficult variables. Wind speed responds to local topography and katabatic structure, while RH combines moisture and temperature errors through a nonlinear saturation relationship. Third, MCAR-trained models fail under block tests because they learn to use nearby observed values that are unavailable during sustained outages.

These failure modes suggest concrete next steps. Station metadata such as elevation, distance to coast, slope, and ERA5-minus-AWS climatological bias could be added as conditioning variables. A probabilistic decoder or ensemble could expose imputation uncertainty, especially for stations with poor ERA5 representativeness. Finally, a future benchmark should include seasonal and event-based error analysis, because winter stable-boundary-layer conditions and coastal transition seasons may produce different ERA5/AWS mismatch patterns.

\subsection{Interpreting the Third-Party Baseline Scope}
The current study isolates two factors under a controlled benchmark: the missingness curriculum and ERA5 conditioning. SAITS and iTransformer are included with and without ERA5 on the same block-missing test configurations, covering two different attention designs: SAITS uses self-attention for bidirectional imputation with missingness-aware weighting, whereas iTransformer uses variate-token attention. The completed block-test transfer matrix remains central because it shows that a model with ERA5 but MCAR training is worse on block tests than a model without ERA5 but with block training. That result is directly relevant to BRITS, CSDI, and other generic imputation methods because many are commonly evaluated under random-missing or mixed benchmark protocols \cite{fang2020imputationsurvey,wang2024imputationsurvey,du2024tsibench}.

The fair ERA5-augmented baselines are deliberately simple, and their outcome is informative. Giving ERA5 to SAITS and iTransformer removes most of the gap to ECBIT; SAITS+ERA5 even has the lowest aggregate mean, although its advantage over gated ECBIT is not statistically resolved in the paired test family. The appropriate conclusion is therefore conservative: the benchmark demonstrates the value of ERA5-conditioned block training, not a definitive ranking of all possible imputation architectures. Broader BRITS/CSDI-style block-curriculum coverage remains the appropriate next benchmark rather than a direct import of random-missing leaderboard numbers.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations follow directly from the evidence. First, gated ECBIT is not significantly better than concat fusion, iTransformer+ERA5, or SAITS+ERA5 in the paired fair-baseline tests. ECBIT should therefore be interpreted as a controlled architecture for studying conditioning and curriculum effects, not as a definitive architecture-dominant solution.

Second, ERA5 is a useful physical prior but not ground truth. The station-month calibration experiment and held-out mismatch diagnostic both show that station-specific and regime-specific ERA5 bias remains important. Because station-month calibrated ERA5 direct substitution is already close to the variate-token neural models, the neural gains should be read as modest incremental improvements over a strong calibrated reanalysis baseline. Other reanalysis products, spatial extraction strategies, station metadata, or adaptive calibration schemes may change the optimal conditioning approach.

Third, the benchmark uses artificial masks on originally observed positions, which provides controlled ground truth but does not directly evaluate recovery inside historical gaps where no AWS observation exists. The real-gap plausibility audit partially addresses this deployment concern by checking boundary continuity around natural gaps, but it is not a substitute for supervised labels inside those gaps. Real naturally missing gaps cannot provide supervised labels, so the reported MAE/RMSE remain simulated-outage metrics sampled from observed positions.

Fourth, station coverage is intentionally selective. The benchmark contains 32 stations that pass record-length, completeness, and metadata criteria. Results may not generalize to stations with poorer completeness, shorter records, more severe terrain mismatch, or variables outside the selected core set. The current variables are temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, pressure, and specific humidity; radiation, precipitation, snow height, and wind-blown snow variables are not included.

Finally, the completed result matrix now includes ERA5-augmented SAITS and iTransformer controls, but it does not yet include every major imputation family under block-curriculum retraining. Extending the benchmark to BRITS, CSDI, probabilistic imputers, realistic-gap surrogate masks, uncertainty-aware outputs, and stronger station-adaptive ERA5 conditioning remains future work.
