\section{Interpretations and discussions}

\subsection{What Does the ERA5 Signal Represent?}
ERA5 conditioning does not act as a uniformly correlated auxiliary channel. Its benefit grows with outage length, reaching a 26.4\% relative MAE reduction at 216 h versus 13.8\% at 24 h, precisely because local interpolation and station-history cues weaken as gaps extend. This pattern is consistent with ERA5 supplying a physically coherent background state that substitutes for absent station evidence during multi-day sensor failures.

The variable-masking analysis further shows that the ERA5 contribution is not uniform across variables. Temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity produce the largest MAE increases when removed. Relative humidity is least important among the tested channels in this benchmark, consistent with the broader result that ERA5 value depends strongly on variable and station context. For the gated ECBIT variant, this supports the decision to condition on ERA5 through missing-variable gates rather than replacing station observations wholesale.

This pattern is also expected from Antarctic ERA5 evaluations: large-scale temperature and pressure evolution is often represented well, while local wind and humidity depend more strongly on surface type, coastal geometry, elevation, and boundary-layer structure \citep{zhu2021era5antarctica,tetzner2019era5antarcticpeninsula,catonharrison2022antarcticwinds}. AWS remains the reconstruction target wherever observations are available. ERA5 becomes useful when AWS evidence is absent, and its usefulness differs by variable.

\subsection{Why Is Fusion Choice Secondary?}
Across gated injection, concat/no-cross fusion, and ERA5-augmented baselines, no single fusion mechanism is preferred by the aggregate tests. Gated and concat variants are statistically indistinguishable (Holm $p=0.553$), and iTransformer with ERA5 is tied with gated ECBIT at 0.258 MAE. SAITS with ERA5 achieves the lowest aggregate mean (0.245), but its advantage over gated ECBIT is not significant after Holm correction. Given aligned ERA5 inputs and block-missing training, the choice of fusion layer is a second-order concern on this benchmark.

The experiments support a narrower conclusion: aligned ERA5 access and block-missing training account for most of the gain in this benchmark. For exogenous time-series models, input alignment and failure-mode design appear to matter more than adding a more elaborate fusion layer. Emerging fusion studies make a related point: auxiliary information often needs constraints, selection, or balancing rather than direct unconstrained mixing \citep{lee2026constrainedfusion,cao2026ecto,chen2025exost}. In this polar meteorological setting, better metadata-aware conditioning, station-specific calibration, and uncertainty-aware ERA5 use are likely to matter more than more elaborate attention modules.

\subsection{Why Does Curriculum Matching Matter?}
ERA5 access alone does not guarantee good block-test performance. A model trained with MCAR masks reaches 0.407 MAE on block tests despite having ERA5 inputs, worse than the no-ERA5 block-trained model at 0.346. The gap reveals an interaction between the external prior and the training curriculum: the model must be exposed to contiguous outage conditions during training to learn how to exploit ERA5 trajectories across multi-day gaps.

For practical Antarctic AWS recovery, this matters more than performance on generic random-missing benchmarks. Station failures often remove contiguous spans of one or more variables, so evaluation and training should match that failure mode.

The completed block-test transfer matrix makes this conclusion quantitative without treating the MCAR rows as in-distribution factorial cells. Under block training, ERA5 reduces MAE by 0.088. With ERA5 present, changing from MCAR masks to block masks reduces block-test MAE from 0.407 to 0.258. This is methodologically useful beyond the present dataset: exogenous variables can be valuable, but their value depends on whether the training mask distribution teaches the model how to use them under the target failure mode.

The same principle applies to any sensor network whose failures are block-structured: polar AWS records, mountain weather stations, marine buoys, and remote ecological networks all tend to fail in contiguous spans rather than isolated points. A model trained on MCAR masks learns a local interpolation problem that is easier than the deployment problem; a block curriculum shifts the task to reconstruction under sustained evidence loss. The quantitative gap demonstrated here for Antarctic AWS records, 0.407 MAE for MCAR-trained versus 0.258 for block-trained under ERA5 conditioning, provides a concrete benchmark for evaluating this shift in other domains.

\subsection{What Are the Practical Deployment Constraints?}
The inference-time temporal-resolution analysis suggests that ECBIT can tolerate moderate ERA5 coarsening after training on 3-hourly inputs. Downsampling ERA5 to 6-hour resolution changes MAE by less than 0.001, while daily inputs are clearly too coarse. This supports practical deployments where only lower-frequency reanalysis products or delayed operational reanalysis streams are available, provided that sub-daily temporal structure is preserved.

For deployments where ERA5 variable coverage must be reduced, temperature, wind speed, and specific humidity should be prioritized: together they account for approximately 84\% of the total single-channel masking degradation (Fig.~\ref{fig:followup-analyses}b), while pressure and relative humidity contribute comparatively little in this benchmark.

The held-out station results further suggest that deployment should include station-level validation. Mean MAE ranges from 0.173 to 0.350 across the five held-out stations, so high-elevation, coastal, or locally complex stations should be checked before operational use rather than assumed to follow the aggregate benchmark average.

Operational use requires quality flags alongside filled values: deterministic MAE/RMSE does not indicate when an imputed value comes from a station with large ERA5 mismatch, and real-time deployment depends on ERA5 product availability, latency, station metadata quality, and preprocessing robustness.

The station-month calibrated ERA5 baseline is also practically important. Its 0.270 MAE shows that a simple train-only station-season bias correction is a strong operational reference. Neural ERA5 conditioning is still useful in the matched tests, but its role is more specific: it adds sparse AWS temporal and cross-variable context on top of the calibrated reanalysis trajectory. For operational users, calibrated ERA5 is the simplest reliable reference, while neural conditioning becomes more attractive when modest accuracy gains, copy-back preservation, and cross-variable consistency justify maintaining a trained model.

\subsection{How Heterogeneous Is Station Generalization?}
Held-out station results vary by roughly a factor of two, from Nico to Mount Sidley. This heterogeneity likely reflects a combination of local geography, elevation, coastal versus interior climate regimes, and station-specific ERA5 representativeness. Mount Sidley has the largest held-out MAE. In the selected AntAWS metadata it is also the highest held-out AWS installation, at 2123 m, on the Mount Sidley volcanic massif. This number refers to the station metadata elevation, not the summit elevation of Mount Sidley itself. The station lies in West Antarctic complex terrain where coarse reanalysis orography and boundary-layer structure can be mismatched to the local AWS footprint. Zhongshan has the second-largest MAE despite its low elevation, plausibly because its coastal setting is affected by sea-ice, ocean, and local wind interactions that are not fully resolved by point-sampled ERA5. Station-adaptive conditioning, metadata-aware calibration, and few-shot adaptation are natural next steps for stations with large ERA5 bias.

\subsection{What Are the Dominant Failure Modes?}
The largest errors occur in three situations. Very long gaps at stations with large ERA5 bias remain difficult: ECBIT can follow the ERA5 temporal trajectory but cannot correct an elevation, coastal, or boundary-layer mismatch without station-specific information. Wind speed and relative humidity are consistently harder than temperature and pressure; wind speed responds to local topography and katabatic forcing, while RH errors compound through the nonlinear saturation relationship. MCAR-trained models also fail under block tests because they learn to exploit nearby observed values that are absent during sustained outages.

Station metadata such as elevation, distance to coast, slope, and ERA5-minus-AWS climatological bias could be added as conditioning variables. A probabilistic decoder or ensemble could expose imputation uncertainty, especially for stations with poor ERA5 representativeness. Finally, a future benchmark should include seasonal and event-based error analysis, because winter stable-boundary-layer conditions and coastal transition seasons may produce different ERA5/AWS mismatch patterns.

\subsection{How Should the Third-Party Baseline Scope Be Interpreted?}
The current study isolates two factors under a controlled benchmark: the missingness curriculum and ERA5 conditioning. SAITS and iTransformer are included with and without ERA5 on the same block-missing test configurations, covering two different attention designs: SAITS uses self-attention for bidirectional imputation with missingness-aware weighting, whereas iTransformer uses variate-token attention. The completed block-test transfer matrix remains central because it shows that a model with ERA5 but MCAR training is worse on block tests than a model without ERA5 but with block training. That result is directly relevant to BRITS, CSDI, and other generic imputation methods because many are commonly evaluated under random-missing or mixed benchmark protocols \citep{fang2020imputationsurvey,wang2024imputationsurvey,du2024tsibench}.

The fair ERA5-augmented baselines are deliberately simple. Adding ERA5 to SAITS and iTransformer removes most of the gap to ECBIT; SAITS+ERA5 has the lowest aggregate mean, although its advantage over gated ECBIT is not statistically resolved. These results support the benchmark conclusion that ERA5-conditioned block training is valuable. A definitive architecture ranking requires broader block training coverage. Extensions to BRITS, CSDI, and related models are a natural next benchmark step; random-missing leaderboard numbers address a different question.

\subsection{Limitations}
The fair-baseline results set the scope of the architecture claim: gated ECBIT, concat fusion, iTransformer+ERA5, and SAITS+ERA5 form a competitive group in paired tests, and the evidence supports the benchmark, curriculum, and ERA5-conditioning conclusions more strongly than a unique gated-fusion design.

ERA5 is a useful physical prior but not ground truth: the station-month calibration experiment and held-out mismatch diagnostic both show that station-specific and regime-specific ERA5 bias remains important. Because station-month calibrated ERA5 direct substitution is already close to the variate-token neural models, the neural gains should be read as modest incremental improvements over a strong calibrated reanalysis baseline. The present benchmark uses station-coordinate point series and does not include ERA5 grid-cell orographic height as an explicit predictor, so lapse-rate adjustment and grid-versus-station elevation diagnostics remain natural extensions for high-elevation sites. Other reanalysis products, spatial extraction strategies, station metadata, or adaptive calibration schemes may change the optimal conditioning approach.

The benchmark uses artificial masks on originally observed positions, providing controlled ground truth for simulated outages. Historical gaps add deployment realism through the boundary-plausibility audit, while the reported MAE/RMSE remain simulated-outage metrics sampled from observed positions because real naturally missing gaps cannot provide supervised labels.

Station coverage is intentionally selective: the benchmark contains 32 stations that pass record-length, completeness, and metadata criteria. Results may not generalize to stations with poorer completeness, shorter records, more severe terrain mismatch, or variables outside the selected core set. The current variables are temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, pressure, and specific humidity; radiation, precipitation, snow height, and wind-blown snow variables are not included.

The completed result matrix includes SAITS and iTransformer controls with ERA5; broader probabilistic families remain a useful next benchmark layer. CSDI, GP-VAE, and related uncertainty-aware imputers would be most informative when retrained under the same block curriculum with fair ERA5 access, rather than imported from random-missing leaderboard settings. The current result files store metrics and best epochs rather than comparable wall-clock traces across remote jobs, so training and inference cost should be benchmarked directly before making operational runtime claims.
